## Core ideas, explained

### 1. The research-seminar payoff: defended work

The oral presentations are the course's assessment philosophy made
visible: students who spent a semester presenting weekly experiments
now defend a capstone in front of the instructor, the teaching
fellows, and their peers. The format — questions as the grading
instrument — is the same one working researchers face, and it is why
this repository's capstone project ([P10](../../projects/p10-capstone-agent-evaluation-and-assurance-dossier/README.md))
requires a defended dossier rather than a passing test suite.

### 2. What a cohort's project selection reveals

The project topics across the iterations are a census of what the
field's newest researchers consider tractable: evaluation methodology,
monitoring, control wrappers, persuasion measurement, agent reliability.
Watching the census shift year over year (Fall 2025's cohort toward
elicitation and evaluation gaming; Fall 2026's toward reasoning and
incident analysis) is itself evidence about where the frontier of
*doable safety work* is moving.

### 3. The METR thread in the closing session

The announced December guests tie the course's two ends together:
Ajeya Cotra's forecasting-and-evaluation lineage (the long-task
measurement that opened the course) closing the loop with the
measurement-heavy semester the students just finished. The pedagogical
arc is deliberate: open with measurement uncertainty, close with the
people building the measurement institutions.
