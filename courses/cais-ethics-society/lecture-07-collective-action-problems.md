## Where this lecture sits

The course's distinctive lecture (the longest in
the series): coordination failures among the humans — races,
commons, moloch — as first-class AI risk, not background noise.

## Core ideas, explained

### 1. The race as the master dynamic

Competitive pressure among labs, nations, and firms compresses
safety margins: first-mover advantages, winner-take-most markets, and
security dilemmas reproduce the arms-race structure at every scale.
The lecture's treatment is the game theory made accessible — payoff
matrices where everyone prefers the safe equilibrium and everyone
individually defects toward the fast one. The repo's
[Ch. 21](../../chapters/21-sociotechnical-and-systemic-risk/README.md)
carries the formal version; every governance chapter assumes it.

### 2. Commons problems in the AI era

The data commons (the Consent-in-Crisis evidence on the Harvard
course's reading list), the compute commons, the attention commons —
each degrades under uncoordinated extraction. And the
safety-information commons (incident sharing, eval disclosure) is
the one this repository's own method depends on: shared evidence
makes everyone's claims checkable, which is why the register
discipline is not bureaucratic ornament but collective-action
infrastructure in miniature.

### 3. Coordination mechanisms, priced honestly

What actually works in analogous domains: verification and
transparency regimes (arms control's lesson: verification precedes
trust), standards bodies, liability allocation, and the
deliberately-simple mechanism of publishing your commitments so
defection is legible. The RSP family (the Harvard lab-vs-field
lecture's subject) is the AI-era experiment in exactly this —
unilateral, verifiable-ish pre-commitment. Its open question,
honest in every treatment on this library's shelves: does
legible self-restraint survive a perceived race?
