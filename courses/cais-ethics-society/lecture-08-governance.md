## Where this lecture sits

The closing lecture: the governance landscape —
domestic regulation (the EU AI Act's risk tiers, sectoral US
agencies), international coordination (standards, treaties,
fora), and the soft-law tools (frameworks, commitments, audits)
between statutes and nothing.

## Core ideas, explained

### 1. The regulatory instrument panel

The lecture organizes governance by instrument: hard law (the EU
AI Act's prohibited/high-risk/minimal tiers with conformity
obligations), sectoral enforcement (existing agencies applying
existing law to new systems), standards (ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF —
the Safeshield series in this library teaches these as practice),
and soft commitments (lab policies, voluntary pledges). Each
instrument trades enforceability against speed and adaptability —
the lecture's map is which tool for which failure family from
Lecture 1.

### 2. The standards layer as the load-bearing middle

Between statute and nothing sits the standards layer — and it is
where most operational safety work actually happens: management
systems, risk frameworks, audit criteria. The repo's
[Ch. 23](../../chapters/23-governance-and-standards/README.md)
teaches the same layer technically; this library's Safeshield
companion is the practitioner course for it.

### 3. International coordination, the open problem

The lecture's honest close: meaningful international coordination
on frontier AI does not yet exist; the precedents (nuclear,
aviation, chemical weapons, ozone) each required a catalyzing event
or a hegemon's interest. The course ends where the DeepMind course's
Part 3 and the Harvard course's regulation lecture end: with the
institutions still to be built — and with the learner now equipped
to read every proposal that claims to build them. The repo's
governance chapter's closing position is the same.
