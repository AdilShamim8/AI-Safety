## What this course is

This is the Center for AI Safety's university course — the video companion to
Dan Hendrycks's textbook *Introduction to AI Safety, Ethics, and Society*
(Taylor & Francis, 2024; also on arXiv as 2411.01042). Where Harvard CS 2881
assumes mathematical maturity and the DeepMind course compresses a lab's
research program into 85 minutes, this course does something neither does: it
teaches AI risk **to everyone** — no calculus, no gradient descent, no
prerequisites beyond curiosity — and it takes the societal and structural
layers as seriously as the technical ones.

That is its distinct evidential value in this library: it is the course a
policy analyst, a journalist, a manager, or a concerned citizen can actually
complete, and it is built by a research organization (CAIS — the same group
behind the Mall Jam protest imagery and the Statement on AI Risk) that argues
for taking extinction-scale risk seriously. Eight lectures, roughly 45–60
minutes each, following the textbook's arc from individual AI systems out to
civilization-scale governance.

## The eight-lecture arc

The structure moves outward in concentric circles — a deliberate pedagogical
decision worth noticing:

1. **Introduction and overview of AI risks** — the map of the whole territory.
2. **AI fundamentals** — just enough of how the systems work to reason about
   their failures (correlational, opaque, brittle, unpredictable).
3. **Single agent safety** — one AI, one principal: specification problems,
   side effects, reward hacking at the conceptual level.
4. **Safety engineering** — the discipline aviation and nuclear built; what
   transfers to AI and what does not.
5. **Complex systems** — when many agents interact: cascades, feedback,
   systemic fragility.
6. **Beneficial AI and machine ethics** — what the *goal* even is; moral
   patienthood and value specification.
7. **Collective action problems** — races, commons, coordination failures
   among the humans, not the machines.
8. **Governance** — domestic and international, hard and soft law.

## How the course organizes knowledge

*The deep-dive version of this section is [pedagogy.md](pedagogy.md).*

The textbook-and-lecture pair teaches in **layers of scope** (agent →
engineering → systems → society), each layer introducing the failure modes
that only exist at that scale. It is also the only course in this library
that teaches **collective action** (Lecture 7) as a first-class topic — the
racing-dynamics material that the technical courses gesture at but do not
teach. Its risk taxonomy (intentional misuse, unintentional failures,
structural risks) is the vocabulary most non-technical policy writing now
uses; learning it here is learning the lingua franca.
