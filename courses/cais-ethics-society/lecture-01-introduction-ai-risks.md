## Where this lecture sits

The course's map lecture: the risk taxonomy that
organizes the entire textbook — intentional misuse, AI accidents
(unintentional failures), and structural risks — plus the
argument for why an accessible, society-scale treatment is needed at
all.

## Core ideas, explained

### 1. The three-family risk taxonomy

The taxonomy that anchors the whole course: **intentional misuse**
(adversaries weaponize capability — deepfakes, cyber offense,
autonomous weapons), **accidents** (well-intentioned deployment
produces harm — specification failures, unexpected interactions,
loss of control), and **structural risks** (no adversary, no bug —
dynamics among many actors produce bad outcomes: races, preference
homogenization, surveillance creep). The taxonomy's analytical value
is diagnostic: each family implies different interventions (security
vs. evaluation vs. governance), and misclassifying a risk buys the
wrong defense. This repository's chapters run the same split —
misuse (Ch. 20), accidents (Ch. 03–08), structure (Ch. 21).

### 2. Why "AI safety" and "AI ethics" share a table

The course's title is a position: the catastrophic-risk community
and the bias/fairness/justice community are answering the same
question (how do we keep powerful optimization from producing
unwanted outcomes) at different scales and time horizons. Teaching
them together — with mutual translation — is the textbook's
distinctive move, and the reason this library pairs it with the
technical courses.

### 3. The accessibility contract

No math, no code — every mechanism explained through worked
examples and historical analogy. The contract matters for the field:
governance decisions about AI are made by people who will never read
a gradient formula, and the course is the vocabulary those decisions
get made in. This note keeps the repo's precision while honoring the
course's register.
